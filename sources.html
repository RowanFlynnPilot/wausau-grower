Where the numbers come from

1. Climate foundation

Hardiness zone: 4b / 5a

Wausau sits on the boundary between zones 4b (average annual extreme minimum of −25 to −20°F) and 5a (−20 to −15°F) on the current USDA map. The tool shows both because the line runs through the metro area, and because a yard's microclimate — a low spot, an exposed hilltop, a south-facing wall — matters more than the map's half-zone.

2023 USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map. U.S. Department of Agriculture, Agricultural Research Service, with Oregon State University's PRISM Climate Group. Based on 1991–2020 average annual extreme minimum temperatures.
https://planthardiness.ars.usda.gov/

Average frost dates: ~May 15 and ~Oct 1

The frost dates are anchored to NOAA's official 30-year climate normals for the Wausau Downtown Airport station (NOAA station USW00014897, "WAUSAU ASOS"). These are the probabilities NOAA publishes for that station:

Why the tool says ~May 15 rather than May 6. A "50% date" is a coin flip: half of all years still see a 32°F night after May 6, and one year in ten sees one after May 23. The tool anchors its spring frost line to ~May 15, roughly midway between the coin-flip date and the one-in-ten date, because a lost tomato crop costs more than a week of waiting. The fall anchor of ~Oct 1 matches NOAA's Oct 2 almost exactly, and the "~139 days" frost-free figure is simply the span between those two anchors (NOAA's 50% figure is 147). This is a judgment call and is presented as one; the underlying figures are above so readers and reviewers can disagree with us.

2. Live forecast and frost-risk flags

The Weather & This Week tab pulls the current 7-day forecast for Wausau (44.9591°N, 89.6301°W) directly from the National Weather Service each time the page loads. Marathon County is served by the NWS Green Bay forecast office. Nothing is cached or edited by us — the numbers on the cards are the NWS numbers.

The tool flags any forecast overnight low of 36°F or colder as a frost risk. That matches the range the National Weather Service itself uses when it issues Frost Advisories (expected temperatures of roughly 33–36°F under conditions favorable for frost), and it is deliberately conservative: valley floors and low yards routinely run several degrees colder than the airport reading.

National Weather Service API (api.weather.gov) — public data, no key required; endpoints /points/{lat},{lon} and the returned /gridpoints/…/forecast. NWS Green Bay forecast office.
https://www.weather.gov/documentation/services-web-api · https://www.weather.gov/grb/

3. Vegetable planting windows

The primary reference for when to start, transplant, and sow vegetables is UW–Madison Extension's statewide planting guide. Its table gives dates for Madison, and its own footnote is the key to using it here:

Vegetable Cultivars and Planting Guide for Wisconsin Gardens (A1653). A.J. Bussan, Judy Reith-Rozelle, and Karen Delahaut. University of Wisconsin–Extension, Cooperative Extension, 2008 (SR-07-2011). Planting-guide table, p. 5, footnote a: "Plant 1–2 weeks later along the lower lake shore and in the central part of state and 2–3 weeks later in northern counties."
https://learningstore.extension.wisc.edu/Assets/pdfs/A1653.pdf

Method. For each vegetable we took the Madison date from A1653, shifted it 1–2 weeks later per the footnote (Marathon County is "the central part of state"), and then checked the result against the NOAA frost probabilities above — pushing frost-tender crops (tomatoes, peppers, basil, cucurbits) toward the later end of the range and letting frost-hardy crops (peas, spinach, brassicas, onions, potatoes) sit at the earlier end. Window end dates and fall succession sowings reflect days-to-maturity counted back from the ~Oct 1 first frost. Varietal suggestions in the guides (Early Girl, Music garlic, Winterbor and Red Russian kale, and so on) come from A1653's cultivar lists and the Wisconsin Horticulture pages below.

Wisconsin Horticulture — Vegetables. University of Wisconsin–Madison Division of Extension.
https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/vegetables/

5. Herbs, flowers, and perennials

A1653 covers vegetables only. For herbs, annual flowers, perennials, and the lilac, the timing in the tool is derived from the frost dates and standard Extension classifications rather than from a single tabulated source:

  • Frost-tender annuals and herbs (basil, zinnia, marigold, petunia, impatiens, dill): sow or plant out after the ~May 15 anchor, with basil and impatiens held to late May / early June because both stall in nights below 45–50°F.
  • Hardy perennials (coneflower, daylily, hosta, chives; parsley as a biennial): planted in the mid-May to mid-June window when soil has warmed but plants can establish before summer heat; hardiness to zone 3–4 is noted per plant.
  • Fall-planted perennials (peony, garlic): September–October windows, per Extension guidance that bare-root peonies and hardneck garlic establish best in fall.
  • Lilac: the planting window and the prune-within-three-weeks-of-bloom rule follow standard shrub guidance.
Wisconsin Horticulture — Herbaceous Ornamentals and Deciduous Trees & Shrubs. University of Wisconsin–Madison Division of Extension.
https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/herbaceous-ornamentals/ · https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/deciduous-trees-shrubs/

6. Monthly task list

The "This month in the garden" checklist is written by Wausau Pilot & Review from the planting windows above and the frost normals — for example, September's "watch nighttime lows" item exists because NOAA's one-in-ten early-frost date is September 20. It is seasonal guidance, not a substitute for the forecast.

7. Our own accuracy review (September 9, 2026)

Before asking anyone else to review the guides, we checked them ourselves: every field of all 31 entries and the monthly task list — botanical names, spacing, days to harvest, water, each planting window, and every factual claim in the descriptions and tips — was compared against UW–Madison Extension, University of Minnesota Extension (the same climate band), other land-grant Extension pages, and, for varietal details, seed-house references. Of roughly 250 checked claims, the large majority were confirmed outright or consistent with Extension guidance. We corrected the following:

  • Garlic — mulch depth was 4–6"; UW, UMN, and UW–Bayfield all say 3–4". Harvest cue changed from a folk rule ("bottom three leaves brown") to Extension's "about half the leaves still green." Softened a claim that all softneck garlic winterkills (some softnecks are cold-suited; grocery-store California types are the problem).
  • Hosta — removed the crushed-eggshell slug remedy (it doesn't work; UW recommends night patrols, beer traps, or iron-phosphate bait) and added deer as the other real pest; hardiness now "zone 3 or 4 depending on variety."
  • Daylily — corrected the cultivar name to 'Stella de Oro'; replaced a recommendation of orange "ditch lilies" with a warning that the Wisconsin DNR treats Hemerocallis fulva as an invasive plant (not regulated under NR 40).
  • Basil and impatiens — cold thresholds raised from 45°F/40°F to 50°F per Illinois, Minnesota, and Michigan State Extension; impatiens now steers readers to downy-mildew-resistant series (Beacon, Imara XDR) or New Guinea types, per UW Extension.
  • Purple coneflower — described as Midwest-native rather than a Wisconsin native (BONAP maps Wisconsin's E. purpurea populations as adventive — native to North America but outside its original range here; Wisconsin's own coneflower is E. pallida, a state-threatened prairie species).
  • Marigold — "pest-discouraging" replaced with the documented deer and rabbit resistance; days-to-bloom corrected upward.
  • Tomato — Celebrity is a mid-season variety, not short-season; indoor start moved to April 10–25 (Extension recommends 4–6 weeks before transplanting, not 8–9).
  • Parsley — indoor start moved two weeks earlier (March 10–25) because UW recommends 8–10 weeks before last frost for a seed that takes 2–5 weeks to germinate.
  • Smaller tightenings — onion curing 2–4 weeks; carrot germination 2–3 weeks; kale's "starch to sugar" wording replaced with the documented cold-raises-sugar effect; chives hardiness and shearing height; sunflower days-to-bloom range; lilac pruning timed to right after flowering per UW A3825; pepper seed-start anchored to the transplant date; pea frost tolerance qualified for flowering plants; broccoli and kale botanical names aligned with Extension usage; the February task list now excludes spring-flowering shrubs from dormant pruning; September no longer says to plant garlic (the garlic guide correctly says October).

Second pass (same day). Two further independent checks were run on the corrected data: a re-verification of every number and date (about 190 fields) against Wisconsin Horticulture articles and the UW bulletins listed below, and a regression check of each first-pass correction. No outright errors remained, but two of our own corrections were imperfect and have been fixed: the daylily note now says the Wisconsin DNR treats orange daylily as invasive but does not regulate it under NR 40 (the earlier "listed as invasive" could be read as an NR 40 listing), and lilac pruning advice now matches UW's Lilacs for Cold Climates ("immediately after flowering") rather than a "within 2 weeks" phrasing that isn't UW's. The same pass fixed internal inconsistencies (pepper seed-start math, zinnia bloom bar vs. its own days-to-bloom, marigold sowing vs. the 65°F soil rule) and tightened numbers to Extension figures: tomato hardening 10–14 days, pepper 60–70 days to green fruit, cucumber 45–60 days, bush-bean spacing 2–4", lettuce 40–60 days (30 for baby leaf), onion sets 3–4", pumpkin spacing stated as in-row and between-row, corn water 1" per week, chives 12–18" and division every 2–4 years.

Two of the three calendar flags are now resolved with Wisconsin's own bulletins rather than an inference: bell pepper transplanting moved to June 5–15 (A3687: "northern growers should wait 2 weeks" beyond the June 1 southern date) and cucumber sowing to June 1–20 (A3688: "not before May 27 in southern Wisconsin… delay planting an additional 1–2 weeks" in the north). The first spinach sowing (April 15) remains flagged for the Master Gardeners, though Minnesota Extension's mid-April date and spinach's 45°F germination threshold support it.

UW–Extension bulletins used in the second pass: A3687 Growing Tomatoes, Peppers and Eggplants in Wisconsin; A3688 Growing Pumpkins and Other Vine Crops in Wisconsin; A3785 Growing Onions, Garlic, Leeks and Other Alliums in Wisconsin (Delahaut & Newenhouse); A3825 Lilacs for Cold Climates (Jull); A2801 Growing Vegetables at Home — Questions and Answers.

8. Review status and corrections

Not yet reviewed by a horticulturist. The plant guides were compiled by Wausau Pilot & Review from the sources on this page and have not yet had an independent review by a horticulture professional. The Marathon County Master Gardener Volunteers (UW–Madison Division of Extension Master Gardener Program) have been invited to review the dates and tips before the tool is promoted to readers; the one item still marked "Review" above (the first spinach sowing) is the first question for them. This notice will be updated when that review is complete.

What the tool does not do. It does not tailor dates to individual yards, account for the current year's weather beyond the 7-day forecast, or cover pests, diseases, or soil testing. For those, the Extension resources above and the Ask Your Gardening Question service are the right place.

Corrections. If a date, variety, or tip looks wrong, email editor@wausaupilotandreview.com. Corrections are noted here with the date they were made.

Code and data. The tool is a single open-source file; the plant database, frost anchors, and forecast code can be inspected at github.com/RowanFlynnPilot/wausau-grower.
