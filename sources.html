Where the numbers come from

Every frost date, hardiness zone, planting window, and forecast in The Wausau Grower traces back to public sources listed on this page, and every planting window is checked against at least two of them. Where we made a judgment call, we say so. Last updated September 25, 2026.

1. Climate foundation

Hardiness zone: 4b / 5a

Wausau sits on the boundary between zones 4b (average annual extreme minimum of −25 to −20°F) and 5a (−20 to −15°F) on the current USDA map. The tool shows both because the line runs through the metro area, and because a yard's microclimate — a low spot, an exposed hilltop, a south-facing wall — matters more than the map's half-zone. By ZIP code, Wausau (54401 and 54403) and Schofield–Weston (54476) fall in zone 4b; Rothschild (54474) and Mosinee (54455) fall in 5a.

Frost dates: ~May 15 and ~Oct 1

The frost dates are anchored to NOAA's official 30-year climate normals for the Wausau Downtown Airport station (NOAA station USW00014897, "WAUSAU ASOS"). These are the probabilities NOAA publishes for that station (in spring, each percentage is the chance of a colder night after that date; in fall, the chance of one before it):

Independent check: Wisconsin State Climatology Office, Marathon County Climate Profile. Average last freeze (32°F) May 6 and average first freeze October 3, computed for 1995–2024 from ACIS station data. That agrees with NOAA's May 6 and October 4 to within a day.
https://climatology.nelson.wisc.edu/marathon-county-climate-profile
Why the tool says ~May 15 rather than May 6. A "50% date" is a coin flip: half of all years still see a 32°F night after May 6, and one year in ten sees one after May 21. The tool plans around ~May 15, between the coin-flip date and the one-in-ten date and just ahead of NOAA's May 19 median for the last 36°F night (the threshold the tool's frost flags use), because a lost tomato crop costs more than a week of waiting. The fall date of ~Oct 1 sits three days ahead of NOAA's Oct 4 median, erring toward an early frost, and the "~139 days" frost-free figure is simply the span between the two dates (NOAA's 50% figure is 150). That is why the tool calls them its last- and first-frost dates rather than averages. The choice is ours, not NOAA's; the underlying figures are above so readers can weigh it for themselves.

2. Live forecast and frost-risk flags

The Weather & This Week tab pulls the current 7-day forecast for Wausau (44.9591°N, 89.6301°W) directly from the National Weather Service each time the page loads. Marathon County is served by the NWS Green Bay forecast office. Nothing is cached or edited by us — the numbers on the cards are the NWS numbers.

The tool flags any forecast overnight low of 36°F or colder as a frost risk. That matches the criteria National Weather Service offices use for Frost Advisories, which are set locally but typically read like NWS Wilmington's: minimum temperatures "forecast to be 33 to 36 degrees" with conditions favorable for frost during the growing season. It is also deliberately conservative: valley floors and low yards routinely run several degrees colder than the airport reading. NOAA's normals put the median last 36°F night at the Wausau airport on May 19 and the first on September 25.

3. Vegetable planting windows

The primary reference for when to start, transplant, and sow vegetables is UW–Madison Extension's statewide planting guide. Its table gives dates for Madison, and its own footnote is the key to using it here:

Vegetable Cultivars and Planting Guide for Wisconsin Gardens (A1653). A.J. Bussan, Judy Reith-Rozelle, and Karen Delahaut. University of Wisconsin–Extension, Cooperative Extension, 2008 (SR-07-2011). Planting-guide table, p. 5, footnote a: "Plant 1–2 weeks later along the lower lake shore and in the central part of state and 2–3 weeks later in northern counties."
https://learningstore.extension.wisc.edu/Assets/pdfs/A1653.pdf

Method. For each vegetable we took the Madison date from A1653, shifted it 1–2 weeks later per the footnote (Marathon County is "the central part of state"), and then checked the result against the NOAA frost probabilities above — pushing frost-tender crops (tomatoes, peppers, basil, cucurbits) toward the later end of the range and letting frost-hardy crops (peas, spinach, brassicas, onions, potatoes) sit at the earlier end. Window end dates and fall succession sowings reflect days-to-maturity counted back from the ~Oct 1 first frost. Varietal suggestions in the guides (Early Girl, Music garlic, Winterbor and Red Russian kale, and so on) come from A1653's cultivar lists and the Wisconsin Horticulture pages below. Section 4 checks every result against other institutions.

Wisconsin Horticulture — Vegetables. University of Wisconsin–Madison Division of Extension.
https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/vegetables/

4. Cross-check: every window, at least two sources

On September 25, 2026, we checked every window in the tool against published guidance from at least two institutions: UW–Madison Extension, and the Extension services of the University of Minnesota (the same climate band), Purdue, Cornell, the University of Illinois, Iowa State, Penn State, and the University of Maryland, plus the Morton Arboretum. All UW–Madison pages count as one institution, however many we cite. The table shows what each source says, converted to Wausau dates; follow a plant's "see what each says" link in the tool to land on its row.

How we converted. Advice given relative to frost ("4–6 weeks before the last frost") is counted from the tool's ~May 15 and ~Oct 1 dates, or from the source's own frost reference where it names one: Purdue's calendar counts from a one-in-ten frost date, which is May 21 and September 21 for Wausau. Seed-starting lead times (weeks indoors) are counted back from the tool's own transplant window, and bloom times from warmer states run a few weeks ahead of ours. Confirmed means at least two institutions support the tool's dates to within about a week. Harvest windows follow from each planting window plus the days-to-harvest figures in the guides (checked against Extension in the September 9 review below) and end at the ~Oct 1 frost for frost-tender crops; bloom windows were checked against published bloom times.

5. Herbs, flowers, and perennials

A1653 covers vegetables only. For herbs, annual flowers, perennials, and the lilac, the timing in the tool follows the frost dates and standard Extension classifications, and each window is checked against at least two sources in section 4:

  • Frost-tender annuals and herbs (basil, zinnia, marigold, petunia, impatiens, dill): sow or plant out after the ~May 15 anchor, with basil and impatiens held to late May / early June because both stall in nights below 50°F.
  • Hardy perennials (coneflower, daylily, hosta, chives; parsley as a biennial): planted from May into mid-June, once the soil can be worked and before summer heat; chives and parsley wait for the ~May 15 frost date, as UW and Minnesota advise for transplants. Hardiness to zone 3–4 is noted per plant.
  • Fall-planted perennials (peony, garlic): September–October windows, per Extension guidance that bare-root peonies and hardneck garlic establish best in fall.
  • Lilac: planted in spring, per Minnesota and Iowa State guidance for container-grown shrubs; the prune-right-after-bloom rule follows UW’s Lilacs for Cold Climates bulletin.

6. Monthly task list

The "This month in the garden" checklist is written by Wausau Pilot & Review from the planting windows above and the frost normals — for example, September's "watch nighttime lows" item exists because NOAA's one-in-ten early-frost date is September 21. It is seasonal guidance, not a substitute for the forecast.

7. Accuracy reviews

September 25, 2026: multi-source cross-check

Every planting, harvest, and bloom window was checked against at least two independent institutions (section 4), and all 31 plants are now confirmed. Four plants changed:

  • Spinach — fall sowing moved from Aug 15–Sep 1 to Aug 1–15 (Minnesota: early August; Cornell: last fall sowing Aug 10 with an Oct 1 frost), so the fall harvest now starts Sep 10. The April 15 spring sowing, the one date our September 9 review left open, is supported by Minnesota, Cornell, and Purdue, so that flag is closed.
  • Kale — sowing moved from May 1–Jul 15 to Apr 20–Jul 10 (Minnesota: mid-April to early July; Cornell and Purdue: mid-April). The old window also couldn't produce the June 15 first harvest with kale's 55–65 days.
  • Chives — planting moved from Apr 20–May 20 to May 15–Jun 10: UW and Minnesota both say to plant after the danger of frost has passed.
  • Hosta — the calendar bar now shows bloom (Jul 1–Aug 31) instead of the whole leaf season (Jun 1–Sep 20), which read as a bloom period.

The same pass corrected NOAA frost figures on this page to the values in NCEI's station file. The earlier version listed Apr 22 and May 23 for the spring 90% and 10% dates; Sep 20, Oct 2, and Oct 15 for fall; 147 and 169 days for the season lengths; and Oct 13 for the 28°F fall date. The tool's own ~May 15 and ~Oct 1 dates did not change, but it now calls them its last- and first-frost dates rather than averages, since NOAA's medians are May 6 and Oct 4. We also added NOAA's 36°F figures, an independent frost-date check from the Wisconsin State Climatology Office, and hardiness zones by ZIP code.

September 9, 2026: field-by-field review

We first checked every field of all 31 entries and the monthly task list — botanical names, spacing, days to harvest, water, each planting window, and every factual claim in the descriptions and tips — was compared against UW–Madison Extension, University of Minnesota Extension (the same climate band), other land-grant Extension pages, and, for varietal details, seed-house references. Of roughly 250 checked claims, the large majority were confirmed outright or consistent with Extension guidance. We corrected the following:

  • Garlic — mulch depth was 4–6"; UW, UMN, and UW–Bayfield all say 3–4". Harvest cue changed from a folk rule ("bottom three leaves brown") to Extension's "about half the leaves still green." Softened a claim that all softneck garlic winterkills (some softnecks are cold-suited; grocery-store California types are the problem).
  • Hosta — removed the crushed-eggshell slug remedy (it doesn't work; UW recommends night patrols, beer traps, or iron-phosphate bait) and added deer as the other real pest; hardiness now "zone 3 or 4 depending on variety."
  • Daylily — corrected the cultivar name to 'Stella de Oro'; replaced a recommendation of orange "ditch lilies" with a warning that the Wisconsin DNR treats Hemerocallis fulva as an invasive plant (not regulated under NR 40).
  • Basil and impatiens — cold thresholds raised from 45°F/40°F to 50°F per Illinois, Minnesota, and Michigan State Extension; impatiens now steers readers to downy-mildew-resistant series (Beacon, Imara XDR) or New Guinea types, per UW Extension.
  • Purple coneflower — described as Midwest-native rather than a Wisconsin native (BONAP maps Wisconsin's E. purpurea populations as adventive — native to North America but outside its original range here; Wisconsin's own coneflower is E. pallida, a state-threatened prairie species).
  • Marigold — "pest-discouraging" replaced with the documented deer and rabbit resistance; days-to-bloom corrected upward.
  • Tomato — Celebrity is a mid-season variety, not short-season; indoor start moved to April 10–25 (Extension recommends 4–6 weeks before transplanting, not 8–9).
  • Parsley — indoor start moved two weeks earlier (March 10–25) because UW recommends 8–10 weeks before last frost for a seed that takes 2–5 weeks to germinate.
  • Smaller tightenings — onion curing 2–4 weeks; carrot germination 2–3 weeks; kale's "starch to sugar" wording replaced with the documented cold-raises-sugar effect; chives hardiness and shearing height; sunflower days-to-bloom range; lilac pruning timed to right after flowering per UW A3825; pepper seed-start anchored to the transplant date; pea frost tolerance qualified for flowering plants; broccoli and kale botanical names aligned with Extension usage; the February task list now excludes spring-flowering shrubs from dormant pruning; September no longer says to plant garlic (the garlic guide correctly says October).

Second pass (same day). Two further independent checks were run on the corrected data: a re-verification of every number and date (about 190 fields) against Wisconsin Horticulture articles and the UW bulletins listed below, and a regression check of each first-pass correction. No outright errors remained, but two of our own corrections were imperfect and have been fixed: the daylily note now says the Wisconsin DNR treats orange daylily as invasive but does not regulate it under NR 40 (the earlier "listed as invasive" could be read as an NR 40 listing), and lilac pruning advice now matches UW's Lilacs for Cold Climates ("immediately after flowering") rather than a "within 2 weeks" phrasing that isn't UW's. The same pass fixed internal inconsistencies (pepper seed-start math, zinnia bloom bar vs. its own days-to-bloom, marigold sowing vs. the 65°F soil rule) and tightened numbers to Extension figures: tomato hardening 10–14 days, pepper 60–70 days to green fruit, cucumber 45–60 days, bush-bean spacing 2–4", lettuce 40–60 days (30 for baby leaf), onion sets 3–4", pumpkin spacing stated as in-row and between-row, corn water 1" per week, chives 12–18" and division every 2–4 years.

Two of the three calendar flags were resolved with Wisconsin's own bulletins rather than an inference: bell pepper transplanting moved to June 5–15 (A3687: "northern growers should wait 2 weeks" beyond the June 1 southern date) and cucumber sowing to June 1–20 (A3688: "not before May 27 in southern Wisconsin… delay planting an additional 1–2 weeks" in the north). The first spinach sowing (April 15) stayed open until the September 25 cross-check above confirmed it.

8. Standards and corrections

Our standard. Every date in the tool must be supported by at least two independent published sources before it appears, and the table in section 4 shows which ones. The guides are compiled by Wausau Pilot & Review. They are not reviewed or endorsed by UW–Madison Extension, the Master Gardener Program, or any other source cited here, and the tool's timing choices, such as planning around ~May 15, are ours.

Adding plants. The plant list will grow, starting with flowers. Suggestions are welcome at editor@wausaupilotandreview.com; a new plant goes in only after each of its windows clears the same two-source check.

What the tool does not do. It does not tailor dates to individual yards, account for the current year's weather beyond the 7-day forecast, or cover pests, diseases, or soil testing. For those, the Extension resources above and the Ask Your Gardening Question service are the right place.

Corrections. If a date, variety, or tip looks wrong, email editor@wausaupilotandreview.com. Corrections are logged in section 7 with the date they were made.

Code and data. The tool is a single open-source file; the plant database, frost anchors, and forecast code can be inspected at github.com/RowanFlynnPilot/wausau-grower.
